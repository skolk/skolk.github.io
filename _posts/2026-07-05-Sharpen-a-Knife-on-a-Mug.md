Flip a mug over and the raw, unglazed ring on the bottom is a sharpening stone you already own. It is coarse ceramic, which is most of what a real stone is. This is the trick I reach for when there is no stone in the drawer, which on a boat is always.

The whole method fits in four panels.

<img src="/images/blog_posts/knife-sharpening-mug-comic.svg" alt="Four-panel instructional comic. Panel one: flip a mug upside down, the unglazed ring is your stone. Panel two: find the angle, start straight up at 90 degrees, halve to 45, halve again to about 23 degrees, so the edge sits almost flat. Panel three: the countdown strokes, pull the sharp side backwards at 45 degrees to the blade, 20 strokes per side then 19, 18, down to 1, with firm finger pressure raising a burr of tiny metal curls. Panel four: wipe, test, then slice, gray metal dust means it is working, then test on a fingernail held at 30 degrees, a sharp edge digs in and stays put, a dull one slides down." style="width:100%;max-width:420px;display:block;margin:1.5em auto;">

Two things make or break it. The angle: start with the blade straight up, halve it to forty-five, halve again, and you land around twenty-three degrees, which is about where a kitchen edge wants to be. And the direction: pull the sharp edge *backwards* along the ring, trailing, never into the ceramic. You are dragging the edge away from itself, not carving the mug.

The test at the end is the part worth keeping. Hold the knife straight up on a fingernail tilted at thirty degrees. A sharp tip catches and stays put. A dull one slides down the nail. It tells you the truth in a second, and it works on any blade, sharpened on a mug or not.
