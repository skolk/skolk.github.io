The engine is the thing that lets you be lazy about your margins. It is the "just in case" you keep in your back pocket, the reason you feel fine cutting a tack near the rocks or leaving an anchorage in a dying breeze. Take it away and every one of those calls comes back to you. We took ours away by accident, partway through a season in the Sea of Cortez, and spent the next week relearning something we had somehow let ourselves forget: *Petrichor* is a sailboat.

<img src="/images/blog_posts/instagram/18118665598291803.webp" width="100%" alt="Petrichor underway in the Sea of Cortez before the engine failure">

We had sailed down for the Baja Ha-Ha the season before, then come back the next year to keep going. This time we took her out of Loreto after a long stretch of boat work: tracking oil leaks, fiberglass, wood projects, hoisting jack lines, fixing the burgee line that had come down the season before. Loreto is a good place to be stuck for a month. The fishermen nap, musicians wander the streets looking for a corner to play on, and I got more writing done at cafes than I had in a while. We were not far from where Steinbeck and Ed Ricketts collected specimens on the trip that became *[The Log from the Sea of Cortez](https://www.goodreads.com/search?q=The+Log+from+the+Sea+of+Cortez+Steinbeck)*, and being in that water put the same idea in my head that I keep coming back to: the people who spend all their time out here should be giving something back to it.

The plan was to head across toward the mainland, a week or more of it, San Carlos and then back down. We got the boat ready, splashed her after all the rework, sat at the dock, and sent her in without a hitch. No issues, which felt like a win after the year before. We anchored out overnight and set an early alarm to push further north.

## 2am

At 2am we woke, raised anchor, and motored out. I took first watch as the moon came up and dolphins worked the dark water around the bow. It was almost Kate's turn when the squeal started, a horrible mechanical squeal from below. The main was already up, so I killed the engine and we hove to. We were in a big channel, two to three miles off the land, which is exactly where you want the room to be when this happens.

I took the cover off. The V-belt was screaming, and the engine was reading 240 degrees against a max operating temp of 190. I sprayed everything down with water and it flashed straight off as steam. Heat was the problem. The raw water impeller looked fine, and water was still coming out the exhaust when I bumped the engine, so cooling from the sea was working. I had a spare belt for exactly this and was about to fit it, but first I went pulley by pulley and found one that would not spin at all.

The manual named it: the internal freshwater impeller, the pump that pushes the antifreeze around to carry heat off the engine. I got the coolant out with a trash bag and a funnel, pulled the part, and found I could only turn it by hand with real force. A bearing had gone off-kilter and the whole thing was seizing. I had over-tightened the V-belt, and that side load had shifted a bearing until the pump locked. We had no spare for that one. By six in the morning it was plain: no freshwater pump meant no cooling, which meant no engine, on a boat far from any chandlery that would carry the part.

That left the choices stark. We could turn back to Loreto and effectively spend the season there. We could sail down to La Paz and fix it. We could, in truth, sail anywhere we wanted, which was the whole point of owning a sailboat in the first place. Kate told me to come watch the sunrise and take a nap. We turned south, the long way, back the way we had come.

## Sailing on what you have

Under headsail alone the boat is slow, and slow is the condition you have to make peace with. So we flew everything. We ran the spinnaker in the light stuff between Los Gatos and San Evaristo, holding her tight, trimming more than jibing, until the wind built past what we wanted and the jibe we had been dodging for four hours turned into the usual chaos with two people on the halyards. Somewhere in there my hat blew off the back. We ran a man-overboard drill for a hat, under spinnaker, with no engine to tuck in behind as insurance, and got it on the third pass. I was honestly amazed the thing was still floating after a fifteen-knot blow in that chop.

The lessons stacked up in the anchorages more than the passages. You come in under sail, you get pushed back by the wind, and you learn to give up any anchorage you cannot afford to be wrong about. No lee shores. If we started to drag with no motor, there was nothing to do about it, so a cove with rocks downwind of us was simply off the list, however pretty. It teaches patience, and it teaches you which margins are real and which ones you had only been renting from the engine. Kate anchored under sail for the first time coming into Puente Colorado behind Isla Carmen, flapping in irons at dawn with a lee shore just south and a point to clear. We got good at it because we had to.

Between the failure and La Paz we were happy to make three knots one day and surfing nine to eleven downwind the next, when a strait finally handed us the wind. Same boat, same sails, no engine. The difference was entirely the weather and our willingness to take what it gave.

## Forty bucks

We dropped anchor a few miles out from La Paz and I rowed to the beach and taxied into town, chasing the part. I put the problem out on the cruiser net and got sent across the city, marine store to tractor-supply shop, hunting a replacement pump. Eventually a mechanic drove me to a boatyard, and the harbormaster there looked at me like I had said something foolish. Why are you trying to buy a replacement? Go get the part repaired. It's going to be forty bucks.

So I went to the shop. The guy quoted me twenty-five to rebuild it. I asked about a rush order and he said, sure, forty. They rebuilt our freshwater pump in a few hours. I taxied back to the beach, picked up Kate, and by sunset the motor was running and we slept happy.

That is the whole education, really. Half of it was mechanical: the book-and-break spares you did not know you needed, the ones the manual does not put a star next to. The other half was learning that things work differently here than they do in the States, that the answer to a broken part is often not a new part but the guy down the road who will fix the old one for the price of a dinner. And underneath both of those, the simplest thing, the one it took a seized pump to teach us. Your boat can be a sailboat. It was the entire point, and we had forgotten it until we had no choice.
