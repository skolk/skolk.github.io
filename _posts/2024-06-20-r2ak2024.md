The Race to Alaska is the engineless run from Port Townsend to Ketchikan: no motor, no support, you fix what breaks or you are done. We did it because it gave us an excuse to go all the way up, and it was honestly just a great time. A good excuse to see Alaska. It let us leave our jobs, leave society for a while, and see a part of the world you do not reach any other way. It also brought us in with a bunch of crazy people who are now some of our best friends. It puts you in a separate category of sailor. You have either done it or you haven't, and we have now definitely done it.

<img src="/images/blog_posts/r2ak-2024-start.jpg" width="100%" alt="The R2AK fleet at first light off Port Townsend, small boats silhouetted on the water against an orange sunrise with Mount Baker behind">

We raced as Team Natural Disaster on *Wildfire*, an Olson 30 sailed for its owners, Team Monkey Fist. Kate sailed with me.

## The shakedown

We meant to be out Friday to Sunday and in the end only made it Friday to Saturday. We broke a pedal, almost messed up an anchor, and got an amazing Aurora Borealis for our trouble. Coming back, we got past Admiralty Inlet, realized we were zonked and tired, and figured we were not going to benefit much more. We put up a spinnaker for the run home and promptly found a whole new set of problems. That is what a shakedown is for.

## Across to Victoria

The first leg, the crossing to Victoria, we finished fifth or sixth, which is about how we finished the whole race in the end. There were not too many specifics. We did fine, really enjoyed the time over, and then had two or three days to load up on supplies. It felt like more time than we needed, though we used all of it anyway.

## The forestay lets go

We came out of Victoria and, reading the winds and tides, decided to go through Active Pass. That let us catch the wind and start north toward Nanaimo. Around six or seven in the morning Kate and I had just come on watch. We were a little overpowered and probably should have cheated down. Then the headstay went. All of a sudden the mast was held up by a single sail and about to come down, and that would have been the whole race, right there. We got two halyards on it, kept it standing, dropped the sails, and started pedaling.

That slowed us maybe six hours and put us out of any top-three finish. So we kept at it and called in every favor we could, from every friend we could reach, and pedaled to Nanaimo, which took forever. We ended up getting a forestay delivered from Blackline Riggers, probably a friend of a friend of Ballard Sails, and it fit the first time, which never happens. Ev, from Team Sail Like a Girl and We Brake for Whales, motored over to the dock to help us get back together. We sent Kate up the rig. And then we were back on. That was days one and two.

That night gave us one of our roughest stretches, 26 to 30 knots on the nose, on a brand-new forestay. It held.

## Day three, flying

We had passed a lot of boats before the break, and now we had to catch them again. Day three, heading toward Seymour Narrows, we put up a sail and went flying, eight or nine knots, maybe even ten, with twenty showing behind us. We passed almost every boat that had passed us, which put us back on course to be among the top finishers. We zoomed through Seymour Narrows and caught up with the teenagers who had gotten by us, the kids' table. We battled through the night, got caught in a few swirls, and came out neck and neck with either Nomads or Roscoe Pickle Train, Odin and Pearl's boat. We ended up passing both. It got wider in Queen Charlotte, and we came out ahead of some of them.

## The outside gamble

Instead of running the inside to Bella Bella, we went outside, and that definitely put us ahead, because the inside boats had slack winds. We threaded some shallows in daylight and came through Bella Bella unscathed. Somebody threw an onion at us, in a good way, one of the race boats. Then we headed up the outside.

Call it day four or five. It ran somewhere between champagne sailing and dark and rough. At least one rough night, nothing too insane. It was a light-wind year compared to other races. We made our way up the strait toward Ketchikan and saw Mojo once or twice, though they had their own problems.

## Ketchikan

We made it up to Ketchikan. Fifth, I think. There were three serious trimarans that year, all in the same class, a forty-footer among them, and I forget who else. It was a great finish, mostly in high spirits, and I loved it.

The jury rig in Nanaimo was our biggest problem, and sending Kate up the rig was the sharp end of it. The low point was the moment right after the headstay went: oh shit, we are not going to be able to fix this.

Everybody should try it. If you are a racer, if you are a sailor, it puts a lot of your assumptions about what you can do to the test, and it also lets you grow. You have either done it or you haven't. We have done it.
