The Inside Passage is the apprenticeship. *Petrichor*, my 1990 Catalina 36, is a small boat for it, and the run from Shilshole to Southeast Alaska taught me what cruising actually asks: read the tide gates, take the weather window when it opens, and keep the boat and the people in it fed and rested for weeks at a time. We did it in two crews. Remy and David took the first week, up through the Broughtons to Port Hardy. Then Kate flew in, and later Christina, for the long open coast up to Ketchikan and beyond. It was heavy seas inside the passage, a lot of motoring, and barely any sail. By the end, the piece that stuck with me was not any single fjord. It was the shift to the two of us, Kate and I, being able to run the whole boat alone.

## Week one: Remy and David

We left Shilshole in the dark, a little before four in the morning, crew aboard and the boat loaded for two weeks of coastal water. The plan was eighty-plus miles a day.

Day one we crossed the Strait of Juan de Fuca with twenty knots on the beam and three feet of swell, the current carrying us out of Admiralty Inlet and across into the San Juans. We touched ten knots under sail. I had not planned to cross into Canada until I knew the crew and the weather would cooperate, but we made Friday Harbor by noon, called ahead to the Pender Island crossing, and found it open around the clock. We checked in at Poets Cove, rounded to Pender, dropped anchor, and went ashore for land food. Seattle to the Gulf Islands in one day, with sunlight to spare.

Day two took us to Nanaimo. This is where the gates started to become real. We rounded Salt Spring Island with the motor thrumming and Shakshuka going below, soft-boiled eggs and red peppers, and came up to Dodds Narrows, which has to be crossed at slack or close to it. A gate is a current too strong to fight, passable only around the turn when the ebb and flood change over. We anchored to wait, made lunch, and went in slightly early with a two-knot push carrying us through. Nanaimo is the second-largest city on Vancouver Island, and we rushed the grocery aisles for the fresh vegetables we could not carry across the border.

Day three was the long one. Alarms at two in the morning, out into the Strait of Georgia in the dark, bashing into a headwind with a bow watch going full time. The strait has a double flood: one current helping and another running hard against you, and the trick Remy taught me was to watch the water surface and the passing boats and stay in the favorable band, edging in and out of the back-flow. We threaded up through Campbell River and Seymour Narrows, which Captain Vancouver called one of the vilest stretches of water in the world. It can run up to fifteen knots of current and drowned a hundred and fourteen people against Ripple Rock before the rock was blasted flat in 1958. We caught it at three or four knots with us. Any higher and you start to lose control of the boat. Somewhere along the strait we fell in with Ken and his family, also headed north in a Catalina 36, and we anchored that night at a small goat island out in the middle of the passage.

Day four we crossed into the Broughtons through Blackfish Sound. Day five the tides rolled us out toward the open Pacific, complex currents and bald eagles chirping far off, a tugboat surfacing out of the gray and sinking back into it. Cell towers never reached this part of the coast. All we had was the crew, the food we shared, and the occasional book. We made Port Hardy by sunset, pleasantly exhausted, crew turnover set for the next day.

A word on the watch schedule, because it held for both crews. Underway we ran one hour on, two hours off. It sounds thin, but we slept most of the day between passages, so the rotation gave everyone long breaks and kept a rested person always ready to help the one on deck. A crew that learns to rest goes much further than a crew that pushes.

## Port Hardy: the crew swap, and the open coast with Kate

I dropped Remy and David at Port Hardy, and a few days later Kate flew in. From there it was the two of us. We left very early and rounded Cape Caution, the first real exposure to Pacific swell on the trip, and got into Bella Bella very late, ten o'clock or so, anchoring and rafting up wherever we could find room in the dark. The next day we ran the short hop to Shearwater, the port right next to Bella Bella, to sort bugs and fixes on the boat.

Christina flew in and we picked her up at the dock. From Shearwater we made Klemtu, and from Klemtu we wound up through a run of fjords and narrow passages to a hot spring. I cannot remember its name right now, and I will find it later, but there are some great pictures from that stop. Then we turned up toward the strait below Prince Rupert.

That stretch was very foggy, and it was where the batteries started their education. I had to learn all about the house bank the hard way, and by Prince Rupert I had to eat the cost of a new battery. Long epics of batteries, told in installments up the coast. From Prince Rupert we crossed Dixon Entrance and ended up in Ketchikan. That was the trip up: two crews, heavy seas inside the passage, a lot of motoring, and not much sail. I learned a lot from Kate, and it was good to have other hands aboard for a delivery.

## Glacier Bay on a whim

Glacier Bay was not on the plan. As we worked north out of Ketchikan we realized we could point at Glacier Bay instead of the access points further up toward the Yukon. We called in, and they said there was free space. So Kate, Christina, and I took it.

Fifty-nine north, and a fiberglass boat surrounded by little floating chunks of doom. We found a calm mix of fog and ice, sent the paddleboards out into the bergy bits, and made it back without scratching the hull. Out in those islands we met up with other sailors, James and his crewmate in a Catalina 30, and traded notes on ice and anchorages. Lots of hot springs. We went to Pelican. We dropped Christina off in Sitka on the way back, and then it was down to the two of us again.

I could write a whole other piece about going up from Ketchikan and back down to Seattle. That is a different conversation.

## Coming back, changed

What was different on the way home was that we had a lot more diesel miles under the keel. The engine had picked up a few sputters. We had made our peace with the lithium and knew, finally, how it behaved. Mostly we knew how to wake up early and just go. The rhythm of it, that the two of us could handle the entire boat, start to finish, was solid by then. What Kate and I needed after all those weeks was the same thing the trip had been quietly giving us: time and space away from other people, with nothing to do but what was in front of us.
