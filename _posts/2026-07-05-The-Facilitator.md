The [Community Economic Simulator]({% post_url 2026-07-05-Community-Economic-Simulator %}) is about one place deciding its own future. This one is about two places that need each other and do not trust each other, which is the harder and more common problem. It is the other half of the same question I brought home from [Pollica]({% post_url 2026-07-02-Pollica-Gastrodiplomacy %}).

<img src="/images/blog_posts/the-facilitator.png" width="100%" alt="The opening screen of The Facilitator: the title and subtitle two communities, one mediator, twenty turns of weather, an intro panel reading You arrive as a stranger, and the first starting scenario, Even Ground">

[Play it here]({{ '/facilitator/' | prepend: site.baseurl }}).

There is a rural community, Greenhollow, and an urban one, Bastion. You are neither. You arrive as a stranger and sit between them. Each side is its own agent, with capacities, needs, and a disposition: trust, patience, and memory. The urban side has a short memory, values efficiency, and treats the countryside as a backdrop. The rural side has a long memory, values its autonomy, and distrusts being extracted from. Those priors are not decoration. They change how each side reads the same offer.

Every turn a shock lands, and each side decides whether to cooperate or defect. They decide on the payoff matrix, but also on trust, on who broke the last promise, and on whether they feel heard. Your tools are translation, trade, multi-turn agreements, mediation, and shared institutions. The institutions are the Ostrom ladder: a watershed council, a shared market, things that are slow and expensive to build and that permanently change the payoff matrix once they exist. You win by reaching turn twenty with both sides standing, trust climbing, and four or more institutions built. You lose if either side collapses, or if trust hits zero on both.

It is iterated game theory with asymmetry and memory bolted on, Schelling and Axelrod and Ostrom in a form you can lose in an afternoon. Start on Even Ground, with trust at forty and forty-five, and the trap is obvious in hindsight and invisible in the moment: you spend your early turns brokering trades because trades pay off now, and you arrive at the shock that matters with no institutions built and no trust banked to survive it. The cooperation that lasts is the cooperation you paid for before you needed it.

Like the other one, this is a teaching tool for the [engagement work](https://astraeusocean.com/) in Pollica, a way to feel the shape of a coordination problem before standing in the middle of a real one. You cannot script trust. You can, apparently, model how easily it is spent.
