Watch a boat drag its anchor from shore and your mind reaches for the same phrase every time: someone should do something. I have never liked that sentence. It hands the job to a stranger.

Off Whidbey Island in early August, a 32-foot sloop dragged its anchor and started to wander down the channel. We spotted it from the beach. I called the Coast Guard. They already knew about it, and told me to call back if anything changed.

<img src="/images/blog_posts/PXL_20210805_034132517.jpg" width="100%" alt="The drifting sloop seen through binoculars from shore, anchored alone off a forested Whidbey bluff as a powerboat passes">

Nothing was going to change until the boat found a rock or a beach. So I carried my kayak down to the water and paddled out toward it, three or four miles into the dusk, in a two-to-three-foot swell. The swell rolled me once on the way and I wet-exited, then climbed back in and kept paddling. Hauling yourself from a kayak onto the deck of a moving sailboat is its own small problem. I got a rail, then a leg, then the rest of me, and left the kayak trailing on a line.

The engine was the reason the boat was out there in the first place, so I left it alone. I raised the main, fell off onto a broad reach, and pointed her at Langley, about two miles off.

<img src="/images/blog_posts/PXL_20210805_015833065.jpg" width="100%" alt="Looking forward over the bow of the sloop under mainsail, headed toward the wooded shoreline in flat evening light">

I called the Coast Guard back to tell them the boat was under sail and headed for the marina, and they patched me through to the owner, who was off-island. He told me the rest of it. He had anchored there to earn the money to fix the engine, living out of his van. He had been beating to windward for three days before this and still had not reached where he was going. We talked for maybe five minutes. I was single-handing a stranger's boat into a marina I had never entered, in the last of the light, wet and cold, so I kept it short. The Coast Guard and the owner both knew where I was taking her. I was not ransoming a boat, just doing a good deed.

<img src="/images/blog_posts/PXL_20210805_035016293.jpg" width="100%" alt="Self-portrait at the helm at sunset, wet-haired in a life jacket beside the stern rail and an orange horseshoe buoy">

Kate had driven down to Langley. By the time I came in she had found a few locals on the dock, and they rallied. Two of them put dinghies in the water and steered me, in the dark now, to a mooring ball behind the breakwater. We thanked them, lifted the kayak onto the roof of Kate's car, and drove home.

The owner said he would come for the boat the next day. When I went back two days later, it was gone.

I have been sailing San Francisco Bay since 2012, and kayaking, sea and whitewater, since 2007. I learned to sail a Coronado 25 out of the Alameda Estuary with the Washed-Up Yacht Club, and I have raced out of Berkeley, South Beach, Richmond, and with Cal Sailing. Kate and I chartered around Europe, the Indian Ocean, and Mexico before we bought our own Catalina 36 last year. This coming November we are taking her down the coast with the Baja Ha-Ha.
